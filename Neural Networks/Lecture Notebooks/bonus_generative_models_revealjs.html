Discriminative vs generative

Everything so far was discriminative: learn \(P(y \mid x)\) — a label from an image, a redshift from a spectrum.

Generative models learn the data itself — \(P(x)\) — so you can sample new \(x\).

Note

Four families, one notebook. Each is a different answer to “how do you learn to draw samples from a distribution you only have examples of?”

  • VAE — encode to a structured latent, decode back
  • GAN — a generator and a critic in a contest
  • Diffusion — learn to reverse a gradual noising process
  • Contrastive — learn representations, not samples

Why astronomers care: anomaly detection, data augmentation, and simulation-based inference all lean on these.

Part 1 — Variational Autoencoders

A plain autoencoder compresses \(x \to z \to \hat{x}\). The trouble: the latent space has no structure — sample a random \(z\) between clusters and you get garbage.

A VAE (Kingma & Welling, 2013) makes the encoder output a distribution over \(z\), not a point:

\[\text{encoder}(x) \to \big(\boldsymbol\mu,\ \log\boldsymbol\sigma^2\big), \qquad z = \boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol\sigma \odot \boldsymbol\epsilon, \quad \boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\]

That last step is the reparameterisation trick — it moves the randomness into \(\epsilon\) so gradients can still flow back through \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\).

The VAE objective (ELBO)

Training balances two terms:

\[\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\|x - \hat{x}\|^2\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \; + \; \beta \underbrace{\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0,I)\big)}_{\text{regularise the latent}}\]

  • Reconstruction — decode \(z\) back to something like \(x\).
  • KL term — pull every per-example latent toward a shared \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\), so the latent space is packed with no holes. Now sampling \(z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and decoding gives a plausible new \(x\).

Tip

The notebook trains a \(8 \to 2 \to 8\) VAE on three toy “galaxy types” and lets you drag a point through the 2D latent and watch the decoded output morph between classes — the payoff of a structured latent.

Part 2 — Generative Adversarial Networks

Two networks in a contest (Goodfellow et al., 2014):

  • Generator \(G\): noise \(z \to\) fake sample
  • Discriminator \(D\): sample \(\to\) probability it is real

They optimise opposite goals — a minimax game:

\[\min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\log D(x)\big] + \mathbb{E}_{z}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big]\]

\(D\) tries to tell real from fake; \(G\) tries to fool \(D\). At the ideal fixed point, \(G\)’s samples are indistinguishable from real data and \(D\) is left guessing at \(0.5\).

GANs — what to watch for

The notebook trains a GAN on a 1D bimodal target (two Gaussians — think two redshift populations) and snapshots the generator every 200 steps.

Warning

GANs are famously finicky:

  • Non-convergence — the two losses oscillate instead of settling.
  • Mode collapse — \(G\) finds one output that fools \(D\) and emits only that, ignoring the other mode entirely.

Watching the generator distribution evolve toward (or fail to cover) both modes is the whole lesson — the loss curves alone do not tell you.

Part 3 — Diffusion models

The idea (Ho et al., 2020): destroy data with noise in many small steps, then train a network to undo one step at a time.

Forward process — add Gaussian noise on a schedule \(\beta_1 \dots \beta_T\):

\[x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\boldsymbol\epsilon, \qquad \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}(1-\beta_s)\]

As \(t\) grows, \(\bar\alpha_t \to 0\): the image dissolves into pure noise. The forward process has no learned parameters — it is fixed.

Diffusion — the learned reverse

Reverse process: a network \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\) predicts the noise that was added, so we can subtract a little of it and step from \(x_t\) back toward \(x_{t-1}\). Repeat from pure noise \(\to\) a new sample.

Note

Diffusion is what powers modern image generators. The trade-off vs GANs: much more stable to train, but sampling needs many network passes (one per reverse step), so it is slower.

The notebook builds a synthetic galaxy (Gaussian bulge + exponential disc on a \(64\times64\) grid) and lets you scrub the forward noising process to see \(\bar\alpha_t\) in action.

Part 4 — Contrastive learning

The odd one out: contrastive methods learn representations, not samples — and need no labels (self-supervised).

The recipe: make two augmented views of each example. Pull an example’s two views together in embedding space; push different examples apart.

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_i^{+})/\tau)} {\sum_{j}\exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_j)/\tau)}\]

The positive pair \((z_i, z_i^{+})\) is the numerator; every other sample in the batch is a negative in the denominator. \(\tau\) is a temperature.

Contrastive — why it matters

Note

After contrastive pre-training, a linear classifier on top of the frozen embeddings often rivals a fully-supervised network — using a fraction of the labels. This is why SimCLR / MoCo reshaped how large image models are trained.

For astronomy: labels are scarce and expensive (expert morphology tags, spectroscopic redshifts), but unlabelled survey images are abundant. Contrastive pre-training turns that abundance into a useful representation.

The notebook trains a \(2 \to 16\) encoder with InfoNCE on three morphology clusters and shows embeddings before vs after — the clusters pull apart.

Four families, one table

Family Learns Sampling Trains Astro use
VAE latent + decoder fast (1 pass) stable anomaly detection, denoising
GAN generator fast (1 pass) finicky data augmentation
Diffusion denoiser slow (T passes) very stable simulation, high-fidelity gen
Contrastive representation n/a (no sampling) stable label-efficient pre-training
