Your model has an answer. But does it know what it doesn’t know?

Every fit so far has produced a point estimate — a single best value.

Warning

A point estimate is not the whole story.

  • How confident is the fit?
  • What if the data are scarce, or the model is uncertain?
  • How do we encode prior knowledge from physics?

Today: two frameworks for reasoning under uncertainty, their relationship to model fitting, and how they connect to neural networks.

Sampling as integration — Monte Carlo

Many integrals are hard analytically — easy to approximate by sampling.

\[\hat{\pi} = 4 \times \frac{\text{inside}}{\text{total}}\]

Converges as \(1/\sqrt{N}\) — halving the error costs \(4\times\) the samples.

This is the foundation of MCMC — the engine of Bayesian inference, drawing correlated samples from a distribution you cannot write analytically.

First, the vocabulary

You will meet these words all lecture. Plain definitions:

  • Likelihood \(P(\text{data}\mid\theta)\) — if the parameters were \(\theta\), how probable is the data we actually saw?
  • Prior \(P(\theta)\) — what you believed about \(\theta\) before seeing data.
  • Posterior \(P(\theta\mid\text{data})\) — updated belief after the data.
  • MLE — maximum likelihood estimate: the single \(\theta\) that makes the data most probable. It is exactly what \(\chi^2\) fitting / curve_fit gives you.
  • MCMC — Markov-chain Monte Carlo: an algorithm that draws samples from the posterior when you cannot write it down as a formula.
  • Marginalise — integrate a parameter out (average over it) to get the distribution of the ones you care about.

Two interpretations of probability

Two schools of thought. Frequentist treats a parameter as one fixed (unknown) number and asks how often data would look like this. Bayesian treats the parameter as uncertain and puts a probability distribution on it.

The mathematics of probability is the same for both camps. The meaning is different.

Frequentist Bayesian
Probability means Long-run frequency Degree of belief
Parameters are Fixed unknowns Random variables
You get Best-fit + confidence interval Posterior distribution
Prior knowledge Not encoded formally Encoded as \(P(\theta)\)
Core question \(P(\text{data} \mid \theta)\) \(P(\theta \mid \text{data})\)
Key tool MLE, \(\chi^2\) fitting Bayes’ theorem + MCMC

Note

A confidence interval is not a probability statement about the parameter. A Bayesian credible interval is.

Bayes’ theorem

\[\boxed{\;P(\theta \mid \text{data}) = \frac{P(\text{data} \mid \theta)\,P(\theta)}{P(\text{data})}\;}\]

  • \(P(\theta \mid \text{data})\) — posterior: belief after data
  • \(P(\text{data} \mid \theta)\) — likelihood: probable is this data if \(\theta\) is true?
  • \(P(\theta)\) — prior: belief before data
  • \(P(\text{data})\) — normalising constant, usually ignored

Under Gaussian noise: minimise \(\chi^2\) \(\Leftrightarrow\) maximise log-likelihood.

Prior × likelihood → posterior (animated)

The prior (belief before data) times the likelihood (what the data say) gives the posterior — belief after data.

Model fitting: the unified view

Both approaches share the same model and the same likelihood. The difference is what you do with the prior.

The model: a Gaussian emission line on a flat continuum

\[ \text{flux}(\lambda) = 1 + A \cdot \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\lambda - \lambda_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) + \epsilon,\quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2) \]

Free parameters: \(A\) (amplitude), \(\lambda_0\) (line centre in nm).

Prior from physics: \(\lambda_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(656.3, 1.0^2)\) — H\(\alpha\), Doppler-shifted by at most \(\pm 1\) nm.

Worked example: same line, both methods

The H\(\alpha\) line at \(\lambda_0 = 656.3\) nm, amplitude \(A = 0.6\) (the truth). We fit it frequentist (curve_fit → best-fit ± error) and Bayesian (posterior median ± credible interval, prior \(\lambda_0\sim\mathcal N(656.3, 1)\)).

Regime Method \(A\) \(\lambda_0\) (nm)
Faint (SNR 1, 25 pts) Frequentist \(0.71 \pm 0.62\) \(654.9 \pm \mathbf{3.1}\)
Bayesian \(0.47 \pm 0.42\) \(655.5 \pm \mathbf{0.9}\)
Bright (SNR 20, 80 pts) Frequentist \(0.59 \pm 0.01\) \(656.32 \pm 0.02\)
Bayesian \(0.59 \pm 0.01\) \(656.31 \pm 0.02\)
  • Faint: the frequentist \(\lambda_0\) error is \(3.1\) nm — the fit wandered \(1.4\) nm off truth. The prior pulls the Bayesian answer to \(\pm 0.9\) nm.
  • Bright: the two are identical — with enough signal the prior is irrelevant.

Note

Same data, same model. The prior only matters when the data are weak.

When Bayesian wins: the faint detection

When the signal is weak (low SNR — signal-to-noise ratio), the likelihood is broad and the data alone barely constrain the line. The prior anchors the answer where physics says the line must be.

A corner plot shows the posterior: each diagonal panel is one parameter’s distribution; each off-diagonal panel is the joint distribution of a pair (a tilt there means the two are correlated).

Note

What a single best-fit (MLE) misses here: it returns one point — so it cannot show a double-peaked answer, cannot average over nuisance parameters, and at SNR = 2 may lock onto a random noise spike. The posterior shows the full uncertainty instead.

When Frequentist wins: large N

At high SNR / large N, the likelihood dominates. The prior stops mattering.

Three reasons to use MLE when N is large:

  1. The posterior becomes a Gaussian around the MLE (this is the Bernstein–von Mises theorem). With enough data the prior gets overwhelmed — any reasonable prior gives the same answer.
  2. You cannot beat the MLE’s precision (the Cramér–Rao bound sets the smallest possible error bar any unbiased method can achieve — and MLE reaches it for large \(N\)).
  3. Cost: scipy.optimize.curve_fit: milliseconds. MCMC with 32 walkers \(\times\) 2000 steps: minutes. DESI measures 40 million spectra — only MLE is practical.

Note

They converge to the same answer.
The frequentist path is faster and provably optimal when data are abundant.
The Bayesian prior buys you nothing — and costs computation — when N is large.

The convergence in pictures

When to use each

Use Bayesian inference when:

  • Data are scarce (low N or low SNR)
  • You have genuine prior knowledge that should constrain the fit
  • You need a full posterior (marginalisation, propagation of uncertainty)
  • The posterior is multi-modal and MLE would miss solutions

Use MLE when:

  • Data are abundant and the likelihood is well-behaved
  • You need speed (survey pipelines, parameter grids)
  • The prior is vague and would be arbitrary anyway

Neural networks and uncertainty

A trained neural network produces a point estimate — no posterior, no uncertainty.

Bayesian neural networks (BNNs):

Instead of fixed weights \(w\), treat weights as random variables with a posterior:

\[ P(w \mid \text{data}) \propto P(\text{data} \mid w)\,P(w) \]

Prediction is marginalised over the weight posterior:

\[ P(y \mid x, \text{data}) = \int P(y \mid x, w)\,P(w \mid \text{data})\,dw \]

This integral is intractable for large networks — we need approximations.

Types of uncertainty:

  • Epistemic: uncertainty from limited data — reducible with more data
  • Aleatoric: irreducible noise in the data itself

A network that does not model uncertainty conflates the two. It can be confidently wrong in regions far from training data.

MC Dropout

Gal & Ghahramani (2016): leaving dropout on at test time approximates Bayesian inference.

Each dropout mask is one sample of the weights. Run \(N\) forward passes:

  • Mean → the prediction
  • Std → the epistemic uncertainty

\[\text{uncertainty}(x) \approx \mathrm{Std}[\{f_w(x)\}_{i=1}^{N}]\]

Uncertainty grows far from training data — the network knows what it doesn’t know.

When the likelihood is intractable

Everything above assumed you can write \(P(\text{data} \mid \theta)\). For many problems in astronomy, you cannot.

Examples:

  • Gravitational waves: signal model is a full waveform simulation
  • Cosmological inference: connecting \(\Omega_m, \sigma_8\) to clustering requires N-body simulation
  • Stellar population synthesis: a single SED marginalises over millions of stellar tracks

The problem:

MCMC requires evaluating \(P(\text{data} \mid \theta)\) at every step.

If one evaluation costs minutes of simulation time — MCMC is dead.

We need a different approach.

Simulation-Based Inference

SBI replaces the likelihood with a learned surrogate:

\[ (\theta_{\text{sim}}, x_{\text{sim}}) \xrightarrow{\text{neural posterior estimator}} \hat{P}(\theta \mid x_{\text{obs}}) \]

  1. Simulate millions of (parameter, observation) pairs
  2. Train a normalising flow to approximate the posterior directly
  3. Evaluate on real observations — no likelihood required

The neural network is the inference engine.

Note

Cranmer, Brehmer & Louppe (2020) The frontier of simulation-based inference. PNAS 117(48).
